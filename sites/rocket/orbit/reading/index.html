ALTITUDE ≠ ORBIT
READING 1 · MOTION

Orbit Is Falling Sideways

A rocket can cross 100 kilometers above Earth and still fall back. Reaching space is mainly about altitude. Staying in orbit also needs enough sideways velocity.

Gravity never turns off. It pulls an orbiting spacecraft toward Earth every second. However, the spacecraft is moving sideways so quickly that Earth’s curved surface falls away beneath it. The spacecraft keeps missing the ground. This continuous fall creates an orbit.

Near low Earth orbit, the needed speed is roughly 7.8 kilometers per second. A rocket therefore begins mostly upward, to leave the thickest air, and then follows a pitch program that turns the thrust sideways. A very high path with too little sideways speed is only suborbital.

READING 2 · CONSTRAINTS

A Rocket Must Lift Itself

In a free-design model, we can ask for more thrust or a longer burn without paying a price. Real rockets cannot. More fuel adds mass, and the rocket must accelerate that fuel too. Stronger engines and larger tanks also add dry mass.

The rocket’s total launch mass includes structure, engines, propellant and payload. As propellant burns, mass decreases. The same thrust can then create more acceleration. Engineers use the rocket equation to connect velocity change with exhaust speed and the ratio between full mass and empty mass.

The atmosphere adds another constraint. Fast flight through dense air creates dynamic pressure. The highest point is called Max-Q. A good trajectory must gain speed without exceeding the vehicle’s structural limit.

READING 3 · DESIGN

Why Rockets Use Stages

When a tank becomes empty, its metal shell and engine are no longer helping the mission. If the rocket keeps carrying them, the upper engine must accelerate useless mass. A multistage rocket solves this problem by separating empty hardware.

The first stage provides high thrust for liftoff and early acceleration. After its fuel is used, it separates. The second stage then continues with much less mass. This can produce a larger change in velocity from the remaining propellant. The benefit is efficiency; the cost is extra engines, separation hardware and more possible failure points.

Staging does not create free energy. It changes what the active engine must carry. Engineers choose stage sizes by balancing payload, structure, fuel, thrust and reliability. That is why multistage design is an engineering trade-off, not a magic trick.
